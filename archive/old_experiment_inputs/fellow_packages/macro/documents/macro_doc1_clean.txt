Abstract
This paper analyzes the macroeconomic evolution of the European Union member states
that have adopted the Euro, compared to those that continue to use national currencies,
with a specific focus on the Central and Eastern European countries during the period
2018–2024. Using a quantitative and exploratory approach and data provided by Euro-
stat, the European Central Bank, and the International Monetary Fund, we examined a
series of key indicators: interest rates, inflation, GDP per capita, public debt, and foreign
direct investment. The results highlight several macroeconomic advantages for Eurozone
countries, including lower interest rate volatility and a quicker recovery from inflation,
largely due to access to monetary tools such as PEPP and TPI. Non-Euro countries have
experienced more severe inflationary episodes and higher financing costs, which have
negatively impacted FDI inflows. Although some of these countries, such as Romania and
Poland, have recorded solid GDP growth, they remain exposed to structural vulnerabilities
and political and economic uncertainties. Correlation analyses confirm significant negative
relationships between interest rates, inflation, and FDI levels.
Keywords: non-Euro countries; interest rates; inflation; GDP; FDI; convergence; monetary
policy
1. Introduction
For countries considering future accession to the Euro area, the experiences of member
states that have already completed this transition offer important policy lessons. Ensuring
successful accession requires comprehensive preparation, including the attainment of both
nominal and real convergence criteria, alongside the implementation of necessary structural
reforms (Darvas & Wolff, 2023). Such measures are critical to safeguarding macroeconomic
stability and maximizing the long-term advantages of Euro area membership (Campos &
Coricelli, 2015).
The starting hypothesis for this analysis was that Euro area membership provides
a stabilizing framework for managing macroeconomic shocks, particularly during times
of crisis, while non-Euro countries—although maintaining monetary sovereignty—face
greater volatility in key economic indicators. The analysis tracked the evolution of eco-
nomic indicators such as GDP, inflation, interest rates, and public debt between 2018 and
2024 across several EU member states, with a focus on both Euro area countries (Slovenia,
Slovakia, Lithuania, Latvia, Croatia) and non-Euro countries (Romania, Hungary, Poland,
Bulgaria). The Czech Republic was excluded from the primary analysis due to data dis-
continuities and the lack of consistent post-2020 macroeconomic series across all indicators

Using data from Eurostat, the European Central Bank (ECB), the International Mon-

literature; Section 3 presents the data and the methodology used; Section 4 includes the
empirical analysis and discussion; and Section 5 outlines the main conclusions of the study.
2. Literature Review
Comparative macroeconomic research between Euro area members and non-members
has relied extensively on mixed-methods approaches to capture both quantitative indicators
and qualitative policy contexts (De Grauwe & Ji, 2020; Lane, 2023). De Grauwe and Ji
(2020) highlight that Euro membership offers protection against speculative attacks and
interest rate volatility through ECB backstopping instruments. Similarly, Alcidi et al. (2021)
argue that during crises, ECB measures such as PEPP and TPI were critical in maintaining
sovereign debt stability. Yet, critics like Eichengreen and Panizza (2020) note that these
benefits depend heavily on domestic fiscal institutions and reform readiness.
More recent studies (Lane, 2023) confirm that tools such as the Pandemic Emergency
Purchase Program (PEPP) and the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) have acted
as significant buffers against market fragmentation and volatility. Italy and Greece have
benefited massively from PEPPs to reduce the spread of government bonds vis-à-vis
Germany. These interventions reduced market volatility and preserved access to funding
for peripheral countries (Lane, 2023).
Quantitative metrics such as long-term interest rates, inflation (HICP), GDP per capita,
and FDI flows are routinely used to assess convergence and macroeconomic stability
(Baltagi et al., 2007; Égert, 2012; European Commission, 2022). The use of panel data,
group averaging, and Pearson correlation coefficients is supported by recent studies),
which demonstrate that statistical rigor combined with contextual policy analysis (e.g.,
central bank interventions) improves the explanatory power of economic assessments in
dynamic environments.
According to Égert (2012), long-term interest rates in non-Euro countries reflect higher
sovereign risk premia, driven by currency volatility, political uncertainty, and less mature
financial markets. These structural disadvantages often lead to procyclical monetary
tightening, which exacerbates economic downturns and amplifies borrowing costs, as seen
in the inflation crisis of 2022–2023 (IMF, 2023). Long-term interest rates and inflation are
key indicators of nominal convergence, as defined by the Maastricht criteria. Égert (2012)
show that non-Euro countries often bear higher sovereign risk premiums due to exchange
rate volatility and institutional fragility. Neaime et al. (2018) reinforce these findings using
structural VAR models.
Laubach (2009) further demonstrates that in periods of elevated inflation, fiscal risk
becomes more salient in determining sovereign yields. European Commission (2022)
and ECB Bulletins (ECB, 2020b, 2020c; Lane, 2023) confirm that policy credibility and
access to ECB instruments (e.g., PEPP, TPI) mitigate these risks for Euro area countries.
Inflation differentials in the Euro area and among non-Euro countries have been extensivelyEconomies 2025, 13, 239 3 of 25
studied. Angeloni and Ehrmann (2021) and Zdarek and Aldasoro (2009) show that common
monetary policy does not eliminate inflation divergence entirely.
Foundational studies by De Grauwe and Ji (2020) and Campos and Coricelli (2015)
underscore that Euro adoption can enhance fiscal discipline, reduce transaction costs, and
stabilize inflation expectations. These benefits are contingent upon meeting convergence
criteria and sustaining credible institutions.
The recent literature highlights the importance of institutional quality and monetary
credibility in shaping the outcomes of monetary integration. Angeloni and Ehrmann (2021)
emphasize how inflation differentials persist across Euro area countries, influenced by
labor market rigidity, productivity gaps, and sectoral structures.
Moreover, Alcidi et al. (2021) and Eichengreen and Panizza (2020) argue that Eurozone
resilience during crises depends on the interplay between national fiscal capacity and ECB
monetary interventions, such as PEPP and TPI.
Empirical studies using causal inference techniques have gained prominence. For
instance, Klein and Weigand (2021) use cross-country panel regressions to demonstrate
that monetary sovereignty in non-Euro EU countries correlates with increased volatility in
bond spreads.
Huber et al. (2022) apply survey-based identification to assess inflation anchoring.
Poncela et al. (2016) and Elhorst (2014) model macroeconomic spillovers using spatial
econometric frameworks. Their findings confirm that regional policies, monetary shocks,
and investor behavior transmit across borders, shaping country-specific outcomes through
systemic channels.
This literature review supports the central hypothesis of the study: Euro adop-
tion provides a stabilizing framework that mitigates macroeconomic volatility and
supports convergence.
3. Methodology
The study employs a comparative analysis based on a quantitative and exploratory
approach to evaluate, over the 2018–2024 period, the macroeconomic evolution of countries
thatadoptedtheEuro, comparedtoEUmemberstatesfromCentralandEasternEuropethat
havenotadoptedtheEuro. Theprimarymethodologicalapproachisquantitative, involving
the collection and comparative assessment of macroeconomic indicators such as long-term
interest rates, inflation (HICP), GDP per capita (as a percentage of the EU average), public
debt, and foreign direct investment (FDI). Data were sourced from institutional databases,
including Eurostat, the European Central Bank (ECB), and the International Monetary
Fund (IMF).
The selection of countries is based on comparability in terms of economic size, institu-
tional maturity, and EU membership duration. Slovenia, Slovakia, and Lithuania represent
Eurozone members with post-2004 accession, while Romania, Hungary, and Poland are
comparable non-Euro CEE peers.
To facilitate group-level comparison, countries were classified as either Euro area
members or non-Euro states, and average values for each group were calculated and
visualized over time using Python-based data frames and comparative line charts. This
approach enabled the identification of consistent trends and structural divergences.
In addition, Pearson correlation coefficients were used to quantify the relationships
between key variables (e.g., public debt and interest rates, inflation and FDI), highlighting
the extent to which macroeconomic volatility influences investor behaviour and debt
sustainability. This econometric component added depth to the analysis and an additional
layer of statistical rigor.Economies 2025, 13, 239 4 of 25
This paper aims to present the differences in macroeconomic performance between
Central and Eastern European (CEE) countries that adopted the Euro and those that did not,
during the 2018–2024 period. In the context of multiple economic challenges—health crises,
accelerated inflation, and financial instability—the key research question arises: “To what
extent did Euro area membership protect CEE countries from macroeconomic volatility
compared to their non-Euro counterparts?”
Based on this research question, a series of questions is formulated, derived from the
existing literature and grounded in a comparative evaluation of relevant macroeconomic
indicators (long-term interest rates, HICP inflation, GDP per capita as a percentage of the
EU average, public debt, and foreign direct investment—FDI):
Is Euro area membership associated with lower levels and reduced volatility of long-
term interest rates compared to non-Euro countries?
Do Euro area countries experience more stable HICP inflation rates, closer to the ECB’s
target, reflecting better-anchored inflation expectations?
During the 2018–2024 period, did the Euro provide a competitive advantage through
a positive impact on GDP per capita dynamics, FDI inflows, and public debt control,
compared to non-Euro countries?
These observations are explored through a combination of descriptive analysis, corre-
lation methods, and event studies, aiming to highlight systemic differences between the
two country groups and to offer public policy implications relevant to future decisions on
Euro adoption (Romer, 2012).
The quantitative results were complemented by a contextual qualitative assessment
of relevant policy interventions—such as the ECB’s Pandemic Emergency Purchase Pro-
gramme (PEPP) and the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI)—as well as macroeco-
nomic shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic and the inflation surge during 2022–2023.
An analysis of the evolution of government bond yields between the Euro area and
non-Euro countries reveals a significant divergence following the announcement of the
PEPP program. After this announcement, average yields in Euro area countries recorded
a notable decline (from an average of 1.03% to 0.90%), while in the case of non-Euro
countries, the change was negligible (from 1.48% to 1.50%). This difference indicates a
specific stabilizing effect offered by Euro area membership through direct access to ECB
instruments. Thus, participation in the Euro area provides tangible benefits during periods
of financial stress. A major methodological challenge in the comparative assessment of
macroeconomic performance between Central and Eastern European (CEE) countries that
have adopted the Euro and those that have not is the risk of selection bias, stemming from
pre-existing structural differences between the two groups (Puzzello & Gomis-Porqueras,
2018). Countries such as Romania, Hungary, and Poland, which remain outside the Euro
area, experienced increased macroeconomic volatility during the 2018–2024 period, as evi-
denced by higher inflation rates, elevated long-term government bond yields, and greater
exposure to external shocks. However, these characteristics may partly reflect their failure
to meet the nominal and real convergence criteria set out in the Maastricht Treaty, as well
as deficiencies in institutional capacity and economic governance required for sustainable
participation in the Economic and Monetary Union (EMU). In this context, the more un-
stable economic performance of these countries might not be a direct consequence of their
non-membership in the Euro area, but rather an expression of insufficient preparedness for
monetary integration (OECD, 2019).
The comparative analysis relies on a set of core macroeconomic variables, monitored
annually for each country included in the study. Specifically, we selected the following:
Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita (at purchasing power parity) as an indicator of
the level of economic development; annual inflation rate to assess price stability; long-termEconomies 2025, 13, 239 5 of 25
interest rate (government bond yields) as a proxy for financing costs and perceived risk;
and public debt (as a percentage of GDP) to capture fiscal sustainability.
These variables are analyzed comparatively between the Euro area and non-Euro
countries in order to identify systematic differences and to assess the potential role of the
common currency in stabilizing macroeconomic performance.
3.1. The State of Nominal and Real Convergence in Non-Euro Area Countries
According to the Maastricht convergence criteria, the long-term interest rate should
not exceed the average of the three best-performing Member States in terms of price stability
by more than 2 percentage points (European Commission, 2022, Convergence Report, p.
45). In the reference period June 2023–May 2024, these countries were Denmark: 2.6%,
Netherlands: 2.8%, and Belgium: 3.1% (the reference value is 4.8%). The convergence
criteria, represent essential conditions that European Union member states must meet in
order to adopt the Euro. These include the following: maintaining the inflation rate at
a low and stable level, without significantly exceeding the reference value established
based on the best-performing economies in the EU; ensuring that public debt does not
exceed 60% of GDP and that the budget deficit remains below 3% of GDP; the national
currency must participate in the ERM II (Exchange Rate Mechanism) for at least two years,
without significant deviations and without devaluation against the Euro; the long-term
interest rate must reflect economic stability and confidence in the country’s monetary policy,
not exceeding a certain reference threshold calculated at the EU level. These criteria are
designed to ensure a solid and sustainable monetary integration, avoiding macroeconomic
imbalances within the Euro area. Maniatis (2013) also presents the idea that, in many
cases—especially in Central and Eastern Europe—countries have met the nominal criteria,
but have failed to close the real economic gaps with the older member states. This supports
the view that nominal convergence does not guarantee real convergence.

The global economy entered a gradual recovery phase in 2021, yet the legacy of the
COVID-19 pandemic continued to weigh on public finances, labour markets, and inflation
dynamics. As vaccine rollouts expanded and lockdowns eased, most countries saw a
partial rebound in domestic demand, industrial output, and services. The recovery was
uneven, and inflationary pressures began to re-emerge—initially driven by supply chain
disruptions, surging energy prices, and base effects.
In this evolving context, long-term interest rates in non-Euro countries began to adjust.
While still historically low, rates started to reflect early signs of monetary tightening in
anticipation of persistent inflation. In Romania, long-term rates edged down slightly to
around 3.7%, sustained by a cautious monetary stance from the National Bank of Romania.
Hungary’s rate rose modestly to approximately 3%, in line with its central bank’s proactive
response to early inflation signals. Poland maintained a relatively low level at around
2%, reflecting a delayed monetary tightening cycle despite rising price indices. Bulgaria
remained stable at approximately 0.5%, consistent with the discipline imposed by its
Euro peg.
The year 2022 marked a turning point in global economic trends, as inflation surged
to levels not seen in decades, prompting an abrupt reversal in the monetary stance of
central banks around the world. The combination of post-pandemic demand recovery,
persistent supply bottlenecks, and geopolitical tensions—especially Russia’s invasion of
Ukraine—created a perfect storm for price instability. The global energy crisis, amplified
by sanctions and disrupted trade routes, pushed consumer price indices upward across
both developed and emerging economies.
In Central and Eastern Europe, inflationary pressures became particularly acute. Non-
Euro countries such as Romania, Hungary, and Poland were among the most affected, with
inflation reaching double-digit levels by mid-year. Romania recorded a long-term interest
rate of approximately 7.4%, while Hungary’s rate climbed to a staggering 7.5%, the highest
in the region. Poland followed closely with a rate of 6%. That reflected both rising inflation
expectations and the proactive stance of monetary authorities attempting to regain control
over price stability.
In response, national central banks initiated aggressive tightening cycles. The National
Bank of Romania, for example, raised its policy rate multiple times during the year, aiming
to curb inflation expectations and stabilize the currency. The structural vulnerabilities of
non-Euro economies—such as reliance on energy imports, relatively high fiscal deficits, and
weakerinstitutionalframeworks—limitedtheimmediateeffectivenessofsuchinterventions
(Svensson, 1997). Moreover, the local currencies faced depreciation, adding fuel to inflation
via imported goods and energy costs.
Bulgaria, although still outside the Euro area, benefited from its currency board
arrangement, which maintains a fixed exchange rate with the Euro. As a result, long-term
interest rates rose only modestly to around 1.6%, reflecting the perceived lower monetary
risk premium.
Euro area countries experienced a more moderate rise in long-term interest rates,
largely due to the ECB’s credibility and policy lag. Countries such as Lithuania and Latvia
saw their rates increase to around 0.6%, while Croatia, which was preparing to adopt the
Euro in 2023, experienced a sharper rise to 2.7%. Slovakia and Slovenia also saw their
yields climb to between 2% and 2.3%. Although the consequences of the ECB have begun
to show themselves, the single framework has nonetheless given more overall stability.
The year 2023 marked the apex of the inflationary cycle for many European economies,
accompanied by the highest long-term interest rates of the post-pandemic period. Central
banks across Europe had, by now, fully embraced monetary tightening, with cumulativeEconomies 2025, 13, 239 8 of 25
rate hikes and restrictive policy stances intended to curb inflation expectations, restore
price stability, and stabilize volatile financial markets.
Romania’s long-term interest rate moderated slightly to approximately 6.6%, a de-
crease from 7.4% the previous year, suggesting that inflation may have reached its peak.
Nonetheless, this level remained historically high and signalled persistent price pressures
and cautious investor sentiment. Hungary, which faced one of the most severe inflationary
episodes in the EU, continued to struggle with high borrowing costs, as interest rates stayed
elevated at around 7.3%. Poland held relatively stable at 5.9%, balancing between growth
concerns and the need to defend monetary credibility.
Bulgaria, operating under its Euro-pegged currency board regime, saw its long-term
rates rise further to approximately 3.4%. This increase, although lower than in floating-rate
economies, indicated market concerns related to inflation spillovers, energy dependency,
and geopolitical vulnerabilities.
The European Central Bank (ECB) continued its rate hikes into the early part of
the year, but its messaging began to shift toward “data-dependence” and stabilization,
anticipating a possible end to the tightening cycle. Long-term rates in Croatia, Slovenia,
and Slovakia rose to around 3.2–3.4%, while Lithuania and Latvia reached slightly higher
levels, approximately 3.6–3.7%.
Importantly, while interest rates increased across the board, market fragmentation was
lessseverewithintheEuroarea, duetostrongECBsignallingandtheuseofitsTransmission
Protection Instrument (TPI) to guard against unwarranted spikes in sovereign bond spreads.
Countries such as Italy and Spain also benefited from these tools, demonstrating the ECB’s
capacity to act as a backstop in times of volatility—something non-Euro countries lacked.
Inflation began to decelerate, particularly in the second half of 2023, as consumer
demand softened and global supply chains gradually normalized. Romania, Hungary,
and Poland recorded declining month-on-month inflation, even though year-end figures
remained above targets.
Economic activity slowed significantly, especially in interest rate–sensitive sectors
such as construction, real estate, and durable goods. Small and medium-sized enterprises
(SMEs) reported tighter credit conditions, with higher financing costs reducing their invest-
ment appetite.
In macroeconomic terms, 2023 signalled the beginning of a disinflationary phase, yet
one accompanied by new challenges: ensuring a soft landing, maintaining fiscal discipline
amid rising debt service costs, and addressing long-term structural issues such as labour
shortages, productivity stagnation, and demographic decline.
For non-Euro countries, this environment reinforced the limitations of monetary
autonomy in small, open economies vulnerable to external shocks. The volatility of local
currencies and exposure to investor sentiment made them more reactive to global interest
rate cycles and capital flow reversals. Meanwhile, Euro-adopting nations remained more
anchored, thanks to deeper financial integration and the ECB’s stabilizing role.
In 2024, inflation stabilized near the ECB’s target, allowing for cautious mone-
tary easing. The Eurozone’s GDP growth moderated to 1.8%, reflecting a balanced
economic environment.

By 2010, the effects of EU accession became more evident. Romania made an impres-
sive leap, rising from 36% to 53% in just five years. Bulgaria also improved, reaching 45%.
Poland and Slovakia advanced to 63% and 72%, respectively, while Lithuania reached 61%.
This period underscored the importance of EU integration mechanisms, such as access
to structural funds, increased foreign direct investment, and institutional harmonization.
Slovenia consolidated its strong position at 85%, maintaining its status as a front-runner

The momentum of convergence persisted in 2015. Romania climbed to 57%, while Bul-
garia reached 48%, slowly narrowing the gap. Notably, Poland (69%), Lithuania (75%), and
Slovakia (76%) neared the 70–75% mark, indicating that many Eastern European economies
were on a steady upward trajectory. Latvia and Croatia also crossed the 65% threshold,
reflecting improving macroeconomic fundamentals. This convergence phase was strongly
driven by private consumption, export growth, and substantial EU fund absorption.
The years preceding the pandemic brought about accelerated growth for many of
these economies. Romania rose to 70% in 2018 and 72% in 2019. Bulgaria followed with

4. Discussion
The empirical results revealed consistent structural advantages for Euro-adopting
countries, particularly in terms of interest rate stability, inflation control, and FDI resilience.
While all countries were affected by the COVID-19 pandemic and the inflationary shock of
2022–2023, Euro area members demonstrated greater macroeconomic stability and a faster
return to pre-crisis trajectories. This chapter reflects on these findings by exploring their
broader theoretical and practical implications, highlighting the study’s limitations, and
suggesting directions for future research.
4.1. Theoretical Implications
The study contributes to the broader literature on monetary integration by confirming
that Euro area membership enhances macroeconomic resilience during periods of external
shocks. Consistent with the theory of Optimum Currency Areas (OCA), the results under-
score how Euro adoption stabilizes inflation expectations, lowers sovereign risk premia,
and reduces long-term interest rate volatility. These effects are primarily attributable to
the credibility of the European Central Bank (ECB) and the availability of shared mone-
tary instruments such as the Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme (PEPP) and the
Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI). The findings reinforce earlier works suggesting
that Euro adoption can improve fiscal discipline, reduce transaction costs, and offer a
macroeconomic anchor—conditional, however, on achieving nominal and real convergence.Economies 2025, 13, 239 21 of 25
Furthermore, the observed divergence in inflation and interest rate volatility between Euro
and non-Euro countries confirms that institutional quality and the credibility of monetary
frameworks remain central to the success of monetary integration.
4.2. Practical Implications
From a policy standpoint, the study provides important lessons for Central and East-
ern European (CEE) countries that are considering Euro adoption. Firstly, the evidence
suggests that joining the Eurozone can lower borrowing costs and increase investor confi-
dence, as seen in the consistently lower interest rates and more stable FDI inflows among
Euro area members. Secondly, the comparative data highlights that nominal convergence
alone is insufficient. Countries such as Romania and Hungary have at times met the Maas-
tricht criteria in statistical terms, but institutional weaknesses—including limited central
bank independence and fiscal volatility—continue to undermine macroeconomic stability
(OECD, 2021).
Bulgaria emerges as a positive outlier among non-Euro states, benefiting from its
currency board arrangement and prudent fiscal policy. Its example suggests that Euro area
readiness is less about de jure membership and more about sustained policy credibility.
The country’s progress towards full integration offers a model pathway for other non-Euro
states, including Romania and Poland.
In practical terms, governments aiming for Euro adoption should focus on enhancing
fiscal institutions (e.g., independent fiscal councils); aligning monetary policy frameworks
with ECB standards; deepening integration into Euro area governance mechanisms (e.g.,
ERM II, Banking Union); and using EU funds strategically to support real convergence.
4.3. Research Limitations
Despite offering a detailed comparative analysis, this study is subject to several
limitations. First, there is a selection bias risk, as countries that adopted the Euro earlier
may have been structurally stronger or more institutionally prepared than their non-Euro
counterparts. Thus, some observed differences may stem from pre-existing conditions
rather than from Euro adoption per se.
Second, the descriptive and correlational nature of the methodology does not establish
causality. While the study finds clear associations between Euro membership and macroe-
conomic stability, these results could be influenced by unobserved variables such as trade
structure, political stability, or administrative capacity.
Finally, the paper relies on average group-level comparisons, which may mask sig-
nificant intra-group variation (e.g., between Croatia and Slovakia, or between Romania
and Hungary).
4.4. Directions for Future Research
Future studies should consider employing more advanced econometric methods, such
as difference-in-differences (DiD), propensity score matching, or instrumental variable
(IV) regressions, to better isolate the effects of Euro area membership from confounding
structural characteristics (Gravetter & Wallnau, 2016).
It would also be valuable to expand the analytical scope to include the following:
institutional quality indicators (e.g., corruption perception, governance scores); trade
openness and capital mobility metrics; and qualitative case studies of countries at various
stages of the Euro accession path (Ostry et al., 2010). A particularly promising avenue
is a longitudinal study of Bulgaria’s forthcoming transition into the Euro area, which
would allow scholars to observe macroeconomic changes across the pre-accession and
post-accession divide (Moore et al., 2017).Economies 2025, 13, 239 22 of 25
Moreover, integrating social and distributional dimensions—such as income inequality,
employment trends, and cost-of-living adjustments—could offer a more holistic assessment
of Euro adoption.
5. Conclusions
It is worth noting that although similar macroeconomic recovery trends, structural
and institutional differences continued to define the risk premium differentials between
Euro and non-Euro countries. Eurozone members remained sheltered from currency
depreciation and retained access to ECB liquidity facilities, while non-Euro nations had
to rely more on domestic credibility, fiscal discipline, and foreign exchange reserves to
maintain investor trust.
Eurozone countries remained shielded from the volatility experienced by their non-
Euro counterparts. Access to the ECB’s monetary toolkit, including the new Transmission
Protection Instrument (TPI), helped prevent excessive fragmentation of financial markets
and supported debt sustainability in the periphery (World Bank, 2024).
From a macroeconomic standpoint, 2022 highlighted the stark divergence in economic
resilience and market confidence between Euro-adopting countries and those yet to adopt
the common currency. While Eurozone members faced inflation, their adjustment mecha-
nisms were more stable and less costly, due to lower interest rate volatility and stronger
policy anchors. Non-Euro nations, meanwhile, had to grapple with the dual challenge
of taming inflation and defending their currencies, all while preserving fiscal space in an
increasingly uncertain environment.
An integrated analysis of FDI data (2018–2023) in relation to inflation, public debt,
GDP dynamics, and long-term interest rates reveals several key patterns:
• Interest rates and inflation exert a significant negative influence on FDI. When financ-
ing costs rise and inflation erodes returns, foreign capital becomes more selective
and cautious.
• Strong GDP growth does not guarantee high FDI, as seen in Romania and Poland.
Structural weaknesses such as policy unpredictability, corruption, and inadequate
infrastructure deter investors, even when macroeconomic indicators are positive
(Piatkowski & Rzoca, 2022).
• Euro adoption is not a decisive factor for investment appeal. Bulgaria, a non-Euro
country, consistently maintained high FDI through fiscal discipline and institutional
credibility, outperforming many Euro-area peers.
• Policy coordination matter, countries that combined sound monetary policy, fiscal
prudence, and strategic investment in infrastructure (e.g., Latvia, Croatia) attracted
more sustainable and stable investment flows.
A special case is Bulgaria, which is currently in the final stages of the Euro adoption
process. Already benefiting from a currency board that pegs the lev to the Euro, Bulgaria
has maintained a prudent fiscal policy and relatively moderate inflation compared to other
non-Euro economies. Its full integration into the Euro area is expected in the near future
and may offer a valuable case study for evaluating the transition from rigid monetary
regimes to participation in the monetary union. A detailed exploration of this process—
including its implications for interest rates, price stability, and investment flows—could
represent a promising avenue for future research and would enhance the practical relevance
of the present article. Future research should explore causal mechanisms behind observed
trends and incorporate control variables such as institutional quality and trade openness.
Acknowledging the limitations of our descriptive design, we propose that further studies
build on this foundation using more advanced econometric techniques and case studies,
difference-in-differences, to enrich the explanatory power of Euro adoption analysis.Economies 2025, 13, 239 23 of 25

• Active participation in Euro area supervision and financial stability mechanisms—
Croatia joined the ERM II and the Banking Union in 2020, which allowed it to benefit
from direct ECB supervision of its major banks. This contributed to increased investor
confidence and lower risk premiums. Countries such as Romania and the Czech
Republic could accelerate integration into these frameworks before adopting the Euro.
• Strategic use of EU funds for real convergence, green transition, and digitalization:
National Recovery and Resilience Plans (PNNR), such as those of Poland and Romania,
provide opportunities to finance structural reforms and investments in green and
digital infrastructure. These investments can support real convergence and enhance
competitiveness prior to joining the monetary union.
The implementation of these policies would not only help meet the formal criteria
for Euro accession but also strengthen the economic and institutional resilience needed to
ensure that monetary integration is sustainable and beneficial in the long term.
Non-Euro nations are experiencing a confluence of elevated inflation, monetary in-
stability, high interest rates, and inadequate investment, which constrains the rise of real
buying power. The adoption of the Euro might establish a more stable macroeconomic
environment, facilitating steady income growth and a genuine enhancement of the cost of
living in the medium run.
