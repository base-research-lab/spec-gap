1 From solving to certifying
A numerical solver can return a vector, but optimization theory asks a stronger question:
why is this vector optimal? Convexity turns local information into global information, while
duality provides independent bounds. KKT conditions combine these ideas into a compact
certificate.

4 Strong duality and constraint qualifications
Strong duality means d∗ = p∗. Convexity alone does not guarantee it in every pathological
case. A widely used sufficient condition is Slater’s condition: there exists a point satisfying
all affine equalities and strictly satisfying every nonaffine convex inequality.
When Slater’s condition holds for a convex problem, the dual optimum is attained under

mild conditions and the KKT conditions characterize optimality.
Common mistake: Checking KKT without checking assumptions
KKT conditions are always necessary only under suitable regularity assumptions.
They are sufficient for convex problems when primal and dual feasibility, station-
arity, and complementary slackness hold. In nonconvex problems, a KKT point may
be a local maximum, saddle point, or poor local minimum.

This is projection of c onto a triangle. If c is inside, every inequality multiplier is zero and
x∗ = c. If c lies above the line x1 +x2 = 1 but the projection lands in the interior of that edge,
the line constraint has a positive multiplier and the nonnegativity multipliers are zero. If
the projection lands at a corner, two constraints can be active.
This geometric case split is the conceptual basis of active-set algorithms.
7 Sensitivity and shadow prices
Let a parameter b appear in a constraint. The optimal value becomes a value function p∗(b).
Under regularity and differentiability, an optimal multiplier measures the local change in
the optimum when b changes. The precise sign depends on how the constraint is written.

Key idea: Economic interpretation
A positive inequality multiplier identifies a scarce resource. Its magnitude approxi-
mates the objective improvement available from relaxing that constraint by one small
unit.
8 When KKT is not enough
For f(x) = x3, the unconstrained stationarity condition holds at x = 0, but the function
is neither convex nor minimized there. Similarly, nonconvex constrained problems can
satisfy KKT at local maxima or saddle points. The certificate becomes globally meaningful
only when the structural assumptions are in place.
A second warning is numerical: solvers report residuals, not exact equalities. A practical
certificate checks whether primal residuals, dual residuals, and complementarity products
are below stated tolerances.

12 Chapter summary
• First-order conditions use tangent lower bounds and feasible directions.
• Dual variables create lower bounds and encode sensitivity.
• Strong duality connects primal and dual optima under suitable conditions.
• KKT combines feasibility, stationarity, and complementary slackness into an optimality
certificate.
• Assumptions and numerical tolerances must be checked explicitly.
